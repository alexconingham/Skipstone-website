I've been thinking about what I want someone to feel when they finish Remember to Die for the first time.

Not the mechanical satisfaction, though I hope that's there. Not the pride in a good build or a clean run. Something quieter than that. I want them to sit with the ending for a moment and think about the life they just lived through, the fragments they chose, the memories they kept and the ones they let go of. Whether the version they assembled felt true.

Making this game changed something in me that I didn't expect. Writing the fragments meant revisiting parts of my own life with more honesty than I'd applied to them in years. I've always thought of myself as someone who faces things head on. Making RTD showed me a few places where that wasn't quite true. Some memories I'd been carrying had shifted in the retelling. Not dramatically. Just enough.

I don't think that's unusual. I think that's just what memory is. The game isn't a confession. But it came from somewhere real, and I'm glad I made it.

The question I keep getting asked, mainly by myself at 2am, is what comes next.

There's an idea that's been sitting alongside RTD for a while now. A side-scrolling descent through the circles of Hell, inspired by Dante's Inferno but not a straight adaptation. The universe is too rich to ignore: the visual variance between each circle, the characters you encounter, the moral architecture of the whole thing. It lends itself to a game in a way that feels almost unfair to leave on the table.

The mechanic I keep coming back to is Dante's lantern. A persistent item you carry through the descent. Within its light, the world is in full colour, revealing the visual theme of the current circle. Outside the light, everything is black and white. The lantern can be upgraded. It changes the world around you as it does. And Dante's shadow, which the denizens of Hell in the original poem were genuinely amazed by because the living cast shadows and the dead do not, becomes its own evolving system with abilities that develop across the run.

That game would need real artists. Real animation. A budget that RTD might, if I'm lucky, help create. It's not a solo AI project. It's the next stage of the same journey.

For now though: RTD is three weeks from launch. It's the game I spent forty years wanting to make. Whatever happens after it releases, that part is already done.
