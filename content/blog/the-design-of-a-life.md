Remember to Die started with a single idea, and that idea came from psychology, not game design.

I'd been thinking about how we handle memories that don't serve us. As a defence mechanism, adjusting or softening a painful memory is deeply human. It's not dishonesty exactly. It's somewhere between reframing an event to make it liveable and quietly replacing it with a version that never happened. I studied psychology. I knew the theory. Then I started looking at my own memories and found the same thing there too.

That became the game. What if the player had to make that choice deliberately, and live with the consequences?

After every battle in Remember to Die, you're offered a memory fragment: a moment from the life you're reliving. One is marked TRUE. One is marked FALSE. But the game doesn't tell you which is which. Both have lore text. Both feel plausible. The truth isn't always the darker option, and the lie isn't always the comfortable one. You're working with limited information, just like memory actually works.

Three fragments combine at the end of each chapter into a core memory. A pure core, built from three truths, tells you something about the life you lived. A corrupt core, built from three fictions, offers its own kind of coherence. But it's the broken and tainted cores, the ones built from a mix of true and false, that I find most interesting to design. A life that's partly real and partly invented is harder to read. Those cores offer the most ambiguous rewards, and they force the player to actually think about what they chose and why.

The cores carry buffs into the run. Pure tends toward balance. Corrupt tends toward power with a cost. Broken and tainted sit somewhere in between. Experienced players will start to optimise around core combinations. That's fine. The system rewards both the player who follows the story and the one who treats it as a mechanical puzzle.

The age economy came later. I wanted each chapter to feel like a transition between decades of a life, and the seam between chapters became the space where that happened. Ten years pass at each transition. That felt right. Then I started thinking about what it costs to hold onto memories at all.

Taking a fragment costs you three years. Not combat damage, not a stat penalty. Years. Events in life can feel like they take years off it. Choosing to engage with a painful memory, or to bury it under a comfortable lie, is a cost either way. Players who want to see the full story pay for it. Players who skip fragments preserve their lifespan but miss pieces of the narrative. Ignorance is bliss. That felt honest.

The watches came from wanting to make time feel visceral rather than abstract. You start each run with a random lifespan, somewhere between 80 and 120 years. The watch you carry is a unique item that modifies how that economy plays out: some grant additional years, some cost years but offer stat bonuses, and crucially, each watch changes your core stats in ways that can reshape a build entirely.

That creates a real tension. A watch that grants years but shifts your insight into resilience sounds like a good deal until you've spent three levels stacking insight to power your damage-over-time build. Watches are rare. Replacing one mid-run is a genuine decision, not just a loot pickup.

There's one more layer I want to mention. The mementos you collect through a run each carry lore text. Most of it reads as flavour. But players who pay close attention to their fragment choices will find that certain mementos carry callbacks to specific events in the story they've been building. Some of those callbacks are dark. Some are haunting in retrospect. A memento that seemed innocuous in one run tells a completely different story in another, depending on which version of events you chose to remember.

The Bloodied Tenderiser is probably the starkest example of that. I'll leave the rest for players to find.
