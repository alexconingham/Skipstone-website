I want to talk about the part of development that nobody sees and that took longer than almost anything else: making the game fair.

Remember to Die has 32 dice and 112 mementos. Each die has multiple faces with different effects. Mementos modify how those effects behave. Some combinations are obvious. Many are not. A few are quietly broken in ways I left in deliberately. Testing all of that as a single developer, while also building the rest of the game, was not a realistic proposition.

So I built simulators.

Monte Carlo simulations, running millions of randomised combat scenarios across different dice loadouts, different memento combinations, different enemy profiles at different stages of a run. The goal was to find anything that either made the game trivially easy or quietly unfair in ways a player might not even notice until they were frustrated and didn't know why.

What I was looking for was a middle path. The game should feel tough, especially early. A vanilla first run with no unlocked arsenal is a genuine challenge, and I didn't want to sand that down. But I also didn't want the difficulty to feel arbitrary or punishing in ways that weren't the player's fault. There's a difference between a game that's hard because your decisions matter and a game that's hard because the maths are quietly stacked against you.

The dice tier system came out of that process. Common dice are reliable. Rare dice open combinations. Relics shift the game's fundamental logic around a single idea. Legendaries, a handful of them, exist at a level where finding one and building around it is a legitimate strategy and a genuine moment. All of those tiers needed to feel meaningfully different without any single tier making the others obsolete.

The upgrade system helped. Seeing a duplicate die and choosing to keep it permanently refines it, then perfects it on the third iteration. A common die at perfect quality hits differently than a fresh one. That system gave players a reason to commit to a build rather than always chasing the next rarity tier, and it gave common dice a ceiling worth reaching.

The loopholes are intentional. There are combinations of dice and mementos in the game that are, when you find them, obviously powerful in a way that should probably have been patched out. They weren't. Partly because they're rare enough that they never become the dominant strategy. Mostly because stumbling onto something that feels broken and riding it to a win is one of the best feelings in this genre, and I didn't want to take that away from the players who put in the time to find it.

The builds that emerged from all of that testing are more varied than I expected when I started. Thorns builds that punish enemies for attacking you. Glass cannon setups that need to end fights fast or fall apart. Self-damage synergies that make a certain kind of terrible sense. Damage-over-time stacks that make late-game bosses irrelevant if you survive the early rounds. None of them are the only way to play. All of them are viable in the right hands.

Your first run will be hard. That's by design. The arsenal you build across runs, the permanent dice unlocks, the learned patterns, the slowly understood interactions between mementos: those are the game's long arc. The difficulty doesn't disappear. It becomes a different kind of challenge.
